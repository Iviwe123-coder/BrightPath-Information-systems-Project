System Customisation in Higher Education

Higher education institutions use information systems to manage students, courses, staff, assessments and administration. System configuration and customisation help these systems meet institutional needs, but decisions should consider cost, usability, security, staff training and long-term maintenance.

Configuration and Customisation

Configuration means changing settings and options that are already provided by a software system. These changes usually do not require rewriting the software's underlying code. For example, a college might configure its student information system to use its academic calendar, grading scale, course names, user roles and approval process.

Customisation means changing or extending software beyond its standard settings so that it can meet a specific requirement. This may involve custom code, additional modules, specialised reports or integrations. For example, an institution may develop a custom report that combines attendance and assessment information in a format required by its academic department.

Both approaches can help an institution, but they involve different trade-offs:

  • Configuration is generally quicker and less expensive. It often uses built-in options and is usually easier for staff or administrators to maintain.
  • Customisation can meet specialised needs. It may provide a closer fit for unique processes, but can require extra development, testing and technical support.
  • Too much customisation can make upgrades harder. Changes may need to be reviewed or rebuilt when the software provider releases a new version.
  • Both approaches need governance. Institutions should document changes, control access and test important settings before applying them to live systems.

A useful starting point is to configure the existing system wherever possible. Customisation should be considered when a genuine institutional requirement cannot be met safely or effectively through standard features.

Build versus Buy

When choosing an information system, an institution must decide whether to buy an existing product or build a solution itself. Buying may mean purchasing a licence or subscribing to a commercial cloud service. Building means developing software internally or paying a development team to create a solution.

Buying an existing system

A purchased system is often quicker to introduce because the provider has already developed and tested its core features. Commercial products may include documentation, technical support, security updates and regular releases. They can also offer established functions for enrolment, student records, finance or learning management.

However, an institution may have to change some of its processes to fit the product. Licence fees, subscription charges, vendor dependence, data migration and integration costs should be considered before a decision is made.

Building a system

A custom-built system can be designed around the institution's exact requirements and may offer greater control over features and future changes. It can be useful when the institution has specialised processes that available products do not support.

Building can take considerable time and requires skilled developers, testing, documentation, security reviews and ongoing support. The institution is responsible for fixing defects and maintaining the system, and staff changes may create a risk if knowledge is not documented.

Comparing the options

Comparison of buying an existing system and building a system
Consideration Buy an existing system Build a system
Initial implementation Often faster if requirements match the product. Usually takes longer to design, develop and test.
Upfront cost May include licence, subscription and setup fees. May require significant development and testing investment.
Fit to requirements Depends on available features and configuration options. Can be tailored closely to institutional requirements.
Maintenance The provider may supply updates and product support. The institution or its contractor must maintain the solution.
Control Product direction and some features depend on the vendor. The institution has more control, subject to its resources and technical design.

There is no single best option for every institution. Decision-makers should compare the total cost of ownership, security requirements, available expertise, integration needs, accessibility, scalability and how well each option supports institutional goals. A pilot project and consultation with the people who will use the system can help reduce risk before committing to a full implementation.

Change Management and User Training

Introducing a new system or changing an existing one affects people as well as technology. Change management is the process of preparing, supporting and guiding staff and students through a change so that the system is adopted and used correctly.

Good change management should include the following activities:

  • Explain the reason for the change. Describe the problem the system will solve, the expected benefits and what users can expect to be different.
  • Involve users early. Ask lecturers, administrators, students and IT staff about their needs and concerns before finalising the design.
  • Plan the transition. Set a realistic schedule, assign responsibilities, identify risks and decide how existing data will be transferred.
  • Provide role-based training. Teach users the tasks relevant to their roles, such as recording marks, updating student details or generating reports.
  • Offer support after launch. Provide guides, demonstrations, a help desk or designated support staff so users can ask questions.
  • Collect feedback and improve. Monitor problems, gather user feedback and make appropriate adjustments after implementation.

Training should not be treated as a once-off activity. Refresher sessions may be needed when systems change, new staff join or users perform tasks infrequently. Clear instructions and practice using a test environment can help reduce errors and build confidence.

Institutions should also consider accessibility, different levels of digital skills and the needs of users who may require additional assistance. A phased rollout or pilot with a small group can reveal problems before the system is introduced across the whole institution.

Maintenance and Long-Term Costs

The cost of an information system extends beyond its initial purchase or development. Long-term costs, often described as the total cost of ownership, can include subscriptions, hosting, technical support, staff time, training, security updates, integrations, backups and future upgrades.

Before selecting or customising a system, an institution should plan for:

  • Regular maintenance: Apply updates, fix defects and check that the system continues to work as expected.
  • Security and privacy: Review user permissions, patch vulnerabilities, protect backups and follow applicable privacy requirements.
  • Compatibility and upgrades: Test integrations and custom features before changing the system or upgrading to a new version.
  • Documentation: Record configuration choices, custom code, integrations and procedures so that future staff can maintain them.
  • Ongoing training: Budget for new employees, refresher training and significant changes to system features.
  • Support arrangements: Define who handles technical issues, how problems are reported and how quickly critical incidents should be addressed.

A system that appears inexpensive at first may become costly if it requires frequent specialist work or cannot be upgraded easily. Similarly, a commercial subscription may increase over time or charge extra for storage, support or additional modules. Institutions should estimate costs over several years and review contracts carefully rather than comparing only initial prices.

A practical approach is to keep a record of system requirements, prioritise essential features, use standard configuration where it is sufficient, test changes before release and review costs and user feedback regularly. This helps an institution keep its system secure, useful and sustainable.

In conclusion, successful system customisation requires more than changing software features. Institutions need to choose carefully between configuring, buying or building a solution, prepare users for change and plan for maintenance throughout the system's life. Balancing institutional needs with cost, reliability and long-term support leads to more sustainable information systems.
