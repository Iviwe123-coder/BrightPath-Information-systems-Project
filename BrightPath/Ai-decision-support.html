AI and Decision Support

Artificial intelligence and decision support systems help higher education institutions turn data into useful information, identify patterns and make better-informed decisions. When used responsibly, these technologies can improve student support, resource planning and institutional performance.

1. Understanding AI and Decision Support

Artificial intelligence (AI) refers to computer systems that can perform tasks commonly associated with human intelligence, such as recognising patterns, understanding language, making predictions and generating recommendations. In higher education, AI can help institutions analyse large amounts of student, academic and administrative data more efficiently.

A decision support system (DSS) is an information system that helps people evaluate information, compare alternatives and make decisions. It can combine data, analytical models and reporting tools to support decisions that may be difficult to make using manual methods alone. A DSS does not necessarily make the final decision; it provides evidence that managers, lecturers and administrators can use.

AI and decision support are related but are not the same. A traditional DSS may use reports, rules and calculations, while an AI-supported system may use machine learning to identify patterns or predict possible outcomes. For example, a college could use a dashboard to display student pass rates and an AI model to identify students who may need additional academic support.

Why are these systems important?

  • Better-informed decisions: Decisions can be based on evidence rather than assumptions alone.
  • Improved efficiency: Routine analysis and reporting can be completed more quickly.
  • Early identification of problems: Data patterns can reveal issues before they become more serious.
  • Better resource allocation: Institutions can use enrolment and performance information to plan staff, classrooms and equipment.
  • Improved student support: Relevant information can help lecturers identify where students may require assistance.

For example, if an institution notices that a large number of students are struggling with a particular module, decision support tools can help staff investigate possible causes and consider interventions such as tutoring, additional learning materials or revised teaching strategies.

2. Analytics Maturity and Decision Types

Institutions can use different levels of analytics depending on the question they need to answer. Analytics maturity generally progresses from describing past events to recommending actions. These levels help an institution move from simply collecting information to using it more effectively.

2.1 Levels of analytics

  1. Descriptive analytics — What happened?

    Descriptive analytics summarises historical data to show what has already occurred. It uses reports, charts, dashboards and totals to make information easier to understand. A university might use descriptive analytics to display the number of students enrolled in each programme or calculate the pass rate for the previous semester.

  2. Diagnostic analytics — Why did it happen?

    Diagnostic analytics investigates the reasons behind a result or trend. It involves comparing information, examining relationships and identifying possible contributing factors. For example, a college might investigate whether low pass rates are associated with poor attendance, limited access to learning materials or a particularly difficult assessment. A relationship between two factors does not automatically prove that one caused the other.

  3. Predictive analytics — What is likely to happen?

    Predictive analytics uses historical data, statistical methods or machine learning to estimate future outcomes. A student support team might use a predictive model to identify students who could be at risk of failing a module. These predictions are estimates rather than guarantees and should be checked against current circumstances.

  4. Prescriptive analytics — What should we do?

    Prescriptive analytics recommends possible actions based on data, predictions, objectives and constraints. For example, a system could help management compare different class timetables or suggest where additional tutoring sessions would be most useful. Staff should review these recommendations before implementing them.

2.2 Types of organisational decisions

Decision support systems can also be classified according to the level of the organisation at which decisions are made.

  • Operational decisions: These are short-term, routine decisions related to daily activities. Examples include recording attendance, allocating available computer laboratories and identifying students who have missed classes.
  • Tactical decisions: These are medium-term decisions made by departmental managers or academic coordinators. Examples include arranging additional tutorials, adjusting staff schedules and allocating budgets between departments.
  • Strategic decisions: These are long-term decisions made by senior management. Examples include introducing a new qualification, opening another campus, investing in new technology or expanding student support services.

The different levels are connected. Operational data, such as attendance and assessment results, can be summarised for tactical planning and later contribute to strategic decisions about academic programmes and institutional development.

3. Applications in Higher Education

AI and decision support systems have several practical applications in colleges and universities. Their value depends on the quality of the available data, the needs of the institution and how responsibly the results are used.

3.1 Student-success monitoring

Institutions can combine attendance records, assessment marks, assignment submissions and learning platform activity to monitor student progress. Dashboards can display pass rates, identify modules with high failure rates and highlight changes in performance over time.

An AI-supported system may identify patterns that suggest a student needs assistance. A lecturer or student support advisor can then contact the student, investigate the circumstances and recommend appropriate support. The system should be used to offer help rather than automatically label a student as unsuccessful.

3.2 Enrolment and admissions planning

Historical enrolment figures can help institutions estimate future demand for qualifications and modules. Predictive analytics can support planning for applications, registrations and expected class sizes. Admissions staff may also use decision support tools to organise applications and monitor whether administrative requirements have been completed.

For example, if enrolment in an IT programme increases over several years, management can investigate whether additional lecturers, laboratory computers and classroom space will be required. Admissions decisions must still follow the institution's published requirements and applicable laws.

3.3 Resource and timetable planning

Colleges must manage limited resources, including lecturers, classrooms, computer laboratories, electricity and learning equipment. Decision support systems can compare class sizes, staff availability and room capacity to help managers create practical timetables and distribute resources more effectively.

A system could, for instance, help identify periods when computer laboratories are underused or when too many classes compete for the same facilities. Management can then consider timetable changes or equipment purchases based on actual demand.

3.4 Academic performance and curriculum improvement

Analysis of assessment results can help lecturers identify topics that students consistently find difficult. Comparing performance across modules, semesters and assessment types can reveal areas where additional explanations, practical exercises or curriculum changes may be beneficial.

These findings should be interpreted alongside lecturer feedback, student experiences and assessment design. A low average mark alone does not establish whether the problem is caused by teaching, student preparation, the assessment or other circumstances.

3.5 Example of decision support in practice

Imagine that a college notices a decline in the pass rate for its first-year programming module. The institution could follow this process:

  1. Collect data: Gather relevant assessment results, attendance records and student feedback.
  2. Analyse the problem: Compare current and previous results and investigate possible contributing factors.
  3. Identify students who may need help: Use clear, relevant indicators to flag students for supportive follow-up.
  4. Consider possible solutions: Evaluate options such as peer tutoring, extra practical sessions and improved learning resources.
  5. Implement and monitor: Introduce an appropriate intervention and assess whether student performance improves.

This example demonstrates how data, analytics and human judgement can work together. The system provides evidence and possible recommendations, while educators remain responsible for interpreting the findings and supporting students fairly.

4. Risks and Responsible Use of AI

Although AI and decision support systems offer important benefits, they also introduce risks. Higher education institutions must ensure that these systems are reliable, transparent, secure and used in ways that respect students and staff.

4.1 Bias and fairness

AI systems learn patterns from the data used to develop or operate them. If that data contains historical inequalities or does not adequately represent different groups of students, the system may produce unfair results. For example, a model trained on incomplete historical records could incorrectly identify certain students as being less likely to succeed.

Institutions should examine the data, test systems for unfair differences in outcomes and regularly review their performance. Decisions affecting admission, assessment or access to support should not rely blindly on automated predictions.

4.2 Transparency and explainability

Students and staff should be able to understand when AI is being used in a decision-making process and what information influences its recommendations. Some AI models are difficult to interpret, making it challenging to explain why a particular prediction was produced.

Institutions should provide understandable explanations, document how systems are used and establish ways for affected individuals to ask questions or challenge decisions. Important decisions should involve appropriate human review.

4.3 Over-reliance on technology

AI predictions and recommendations can be incorrect. Over-reliance occurs when people accept a system's output without checking whether it is reasonable or appropriate. For example, a lecturer might overlook a student's improvement because a model continues to classify the student as being at risk based on older data.

To reduce this risk, institutions should treat AI as a support tool rather than a replacement for professional judgement. Staff should verify important results, consider individual circumstances and retain responsibility for decisions within their roles.

4.4 Data quality and accuracy

Decisions are only as reliable as the information used to support them. Incorrect marks, missing attendance records, duplicated student profiles or outdated information can produce misleading reports and predictions.

Institutions should maintain accurate records, use consistent definitions, remove unnecessary duplicates and update information regularly. Data validation and routine quality checks can help prevent errors from spreading through connected systems.

4.5 Privacy, security and POPIA

Student records may contain personal information, including contact details, academic results and other sensitive records. In South Africa, the Protection of Personal Information Act (POPIA) establishes requirements for the lawful processing of personal information. Institutions must consider these requirements when collecting, analysing, sharing or storing student data.

Appropriate safeguards include limiting access to authorised users, collecting only information that is necessary for a legitimate purpose, protecting stored information and using secure systems. Institutions should also consider whether a proposed AI service sends personal information to an external provider and whether the relevant processing arrangements are lawful.

4.6 Accountability and human oversight

Institutions should establish clear rules about who is responsible for AI-supported decisions, who can access the results and how mistakes are corrected. Staff members need appropriate training to understand both the benefits and limitations of these tools.

Responsible use requires regular testing, monitoring and review. If a system produces inaccurate or unfair recommendations, the institution should investigate the cause and take corrective action. Human oversight is particularly important when decisions may significantly affect a student's education or future opportunities.

Conclusion

AI and decision support systems can help higher education institutions improve student support, understand academic performance, plan resources and make informed strategic decisions. Descriptive, diagnostic, predictive and prescriptive analytics provide different ways to understand information and respond to institutional needs. However, successful implementation requires accurate data, transparency, fairness, privacy protection and appropriate human oversight. When these principles are followed, technology can support better decisions without replacing the judgement and responsibility of educators and institutional leaders.
